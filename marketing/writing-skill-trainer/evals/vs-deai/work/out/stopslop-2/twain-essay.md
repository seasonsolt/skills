# The Standing Desk

I am fifty-one years old, and my doctor told me to stand more. I took that advice to a furniture store and came home with a motorized "smart" standing desk. It cost 640 dollars. I considered this a fair price for a new life.

The desk comes with an app. The app demands an account. The account demands a password of at least 14 characters. My front door opens with fewer characters than that. I typed the password in with the grim patience of a man filling out a tax form, and the desk accepted me as a member.

On the first morning, the desk rose to its full height of 48 inches and declined to come down. I pressed the button. I opened the app. I logged in again, all 14 characters of it, and the desk stayed where it was, a monument to my good intentions.

It stayed there for three days. During those days I answered my correspondence standing on a kitchen chair, which is one way to follow a doctor's advice to the letter. The chair wobbled. I typed with the care of a man defusing a bomb, and my replies grew shorter. Replies of that length suggest a man in a hurry. I was a man on a chair.

I called the company's help line. A calm voice heard my account of the desk, the app and the 14 characters, then told me to "restart the furniture." I asked what that meant for a desk. The voice repeated the instruction in the same tone, like a flight attendant describing an exit. I have restarted computers, routers and a telephone. I had no experience restarting a chair, a table or anything else that sits in a room and minds its own business. Should I leave it alone for a minute? Should I apologize? I went back to my kitchen chair and thought about it.

My neighbor, Mrs. Abernathy, a retired schoolteacher, came to see what the noise was. She looked at the desk. She looked at me on the chair. She asked me to step down, and I stepped down the way a boy does when the teacher has finished counting. She reached behind the desk, pulled the plug out of the wall and pushed it back in. The desk lowered itself with a small mechanical sigh. She did not ask for the password. She did not open the app. She spent less time on the repair than I spent inventing the password.

I thanked her, and she accepted it with the air of a woman who has been right in front of many children and expects to be right in front of more.

Now I work at the desk sitting down. I keep it at its lowest setting, a height at which it behaves like an ordinary table, and I find that we get along. The app sits on my phone, unopened. I no longer know the password, and the desk no longer asks.

My doctor asked how the standing is going. I looked at the doctor with the steady gaze of a man of honor and said it is going well. The desk and I have agreed to keep the details between us.
