# The Furniture and I

My doctor told me, at fifty-one, that I should stand more, and I did what any responsible man does with medical advice: I bought the most expensive version of it. The desk cost 640 dollars. It is motorized, it is "smart," and it is controlled by an app, which in turn requires an account, which in turn requires a password of at least 14 characters. I mention the password because it was the only part of the system that asked anything of me, and I met the request in full.

On the first morning the desk rose to its full height of 48 inches. This was precisely what I had paid for, and I stood admiring it for some time. Then I wished to lower it, and it declined.

It declined for three days. I want to be fair to the desk here: it did not malfunction in any way I could prove, since a desk that stays at 48 inches is, strictly speaking, doing the one thing a standing desk is for. I had asked for standing, and I was being given standing, continuously, with a generosity that no brochure had warned me about. I decided to cooperate. I carried a kitchen chair to the desk, climbed onto it so as to be level with my work, and answered my correspondence from there, balancing my elbows at the proper height and my dignity somewhere below. It was the most upright I have ever been in my professional life. My doctor, had he seen me, would have been delighted, and possibly alarmed.

In the course of those days I telephoned the company's help line, and a person there gave me the benefit of the company's thinking. I was to "restart the furniture." I have since turned this advice over a good many times. I have restarted a computer, a router and, once, a conversation, but the furniture had never been mentioned to me as a candidate, and I stood on my kitchen chair afterwards feeling that I had been addressed by someone with a different idea of what a chair is.

At the end of it my neighbor, Mrs. Abernathy, a retired schoolteacher, came to see what I was doing on the chair. She looked at the desk, and then at me, and then she pulled the plug out of the wall and put it back in. The desk came down. She did not use the app. She did not, so far as I observed, need a password of any length. I thanked her warmly, and she accepted this as one accepts the thanks of a child who has finally found his other shoe.

I now work at the desk sitting down, at its lowest setting, which I have come to regard as the setting for which the machine was designed. The 48 inches remain available to me, as a country keeps a nuclear option, and I think the better of us both for never using it.

My doctor has asked how the standing is going. I have told him it is going well. This is true in every respect that I am willing to defend, and it is the only part of the whole affair that required no password.
