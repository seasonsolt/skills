# The Desk That Rose

At fifty-one, I did what my doctor told me. He said I should stand while I work, and I took this as a medical order and not a suggestion, because I am the kind of man who obeys doctors and ignores nearly everyone else. I bought a motorized standing desk. It was marketed as "smart." It cost 640 dollars. I mention the price not to complain but to establish how much I had invested in being healthy, which was a great deal, and in being stubborn, which was a little more.

The desk is controlled by an app. The app requires an account. The account requires a password of at least fourteen characters. I want to dwell on this, because I have lived fifty-one years with a perfectly serviceable human brain and never once needed fourteen characters to get a piece of furniture to do its work. A chair does not ask for a password. A bed does not ask whether I am, in fact, myself. Yet here I was on the first morning, inventing a fourteen-character secret to share with a table.

I got in. The desk woke up. It rose to its full height of 48 inches, with the quiet confidence of a thing that has never been told no, and there it stayed. It did not come down that day. It did not come down the next. It did not come down on the third, either, and by then I had stopped calling it a desk and started calling it a monument.

For three days I answered my correspondence standing on a kitchen chair. I should like to say this was dignified. It was not. A man of fifty-one on a kitchen chair, typing at a surface held above his head, looks like someone trying to deliver a speech to a ceiling. I wrote my letters in this position with great speed, mainly because I was in a hurry to get down.

I called the company's help line. A pleasant voice listened to my situation and advised me, with perfect seriousness, to "restart the furniture." I have thought about that sentence many times. Nobody had ever suggested to me that furniture could be restarted. I had always understood furniture to be the one category of object that simply continues. I asked how one restarts a desk. The voice suggested I try the app. I explained the problem with the app. We reached the end of what two people can say to each other.

It was my neighbor, Mrs. Abernathy, a retired schoolteacher, who solved it. She came over, looked at the desk, looked at me on the chair, and with the patience of a woman who has spent a career watching grown people fail at simple things, she pulled the plug out of the wall and put it back in. The desk came down. It made a small sound as it descended, a kind of mechanical sigh, as if it too had been waiting for someone sensible to arrive.

I thanked her. She said it was nothing. She was right, and that was the worst part.

I now work at the desk sitting down, at its lowest setting, which is, I notice, the sort of height a perfectly ordinary table would have offered. I have spent 640 dollars to return to where I started, with the addition of a password.

My doctor has asked me how the standing is going. I told him it is going well. I believe this is technically true. The desk is certainly standing. It is merely my own part in the arrangement that has been left open to interpretation.
