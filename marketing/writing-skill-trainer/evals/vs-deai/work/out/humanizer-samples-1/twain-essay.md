# The Desk That Rose

At fifty-one I did what my doctor told me. He said I should stand while I work, and I took this as an order and not a suggestion, being the kind of man who obeys doctors and ignores nearly everyone else. I bought a motorized standing desk, marketed as "smart," for 640 dollars. I mention the price not to complain but to show how much I had invested in being healthy, which was a great deal, and in being stubborn, which was a little more.

The desk is controlled by an app. The app requires an account, and the account requires a password of at least fourteen characters. I want to dwell on this, because in fifty-one years I have never needed fourteen characters to make a piece of furniture do its work. A chair does not ask for a password. A bed does not ask whether I am, in fact, myself. Yet there I was on the first morning, inventing a fourteen-character secret to share with a table.

I got in. The desk woke up and rose to its full height of 48 inches, with the quiet confidence of a thing that has never been told no, and there it stayed. It did not come down that day, or the next, or the third, and by then I had stopped calling it a desk and started calling it a monument.

For three days I answered my correspondence standing on a kitchen chair. I should like to say this was dignified. It was not. A man of fifty-one on a kitchen chair, typing at a surface held above his head, looks like someone delivering a speech to a ceiling. I wrote my letters with great speed, mainly because I was in a hurry to get down.

I called the company's help line. A pleasant voice listened to my situation and advised me, with perfect seriousness, to "restart the furniture." I have thought about that sentence many times. I had always understood furniture to be the one category of object that simply continues. I asked how one restarts a desk. The voice suggested the app. I explained the trouble with the app. We reached the end of what two people can say to each other.

It was my neighbor, Mrs. Abernathy, a retired schoolteacher, who solved it. She came over, looked at the desk, looked at me on the chair, and with the patience of a woman who has spent a career watching grown people fail at simple things, pulled the plug out of the wall and put it back in. The desk came down with a small mechanical sigh, as if it too had been waiting for someone sensible to arrive.

I thanked her. She said it was nothing. She was right, and that was the worst part.

I now work at the desk sitting down, at its lowest setting, which is the height any ordinary table would have offered. I have spent 640 dollars to return to where I started, with the addition of a password.

My doctor has asked me how the standing is going. I told him it is going well. I believe this is technically true. The desk is certainly standing, and only my own part in the arrangement has been left open to interpretation.
