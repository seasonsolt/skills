# The Desk That Stood Up

My doctor, who is a persuasive man, told me that at fifty-one I should stop sitting all day. I took this as a medical order and bought a motorized "smart" standing desk for 640 dollars. I did not buy it because I wanted to stand. I bought it because a man in a white coat said the word "should" and I am, at heart, a coward.

The desk is controlled by an app. The app requires an account. The account requires a password of at least fourteen characters. Many dogs have shorter names than that. I invented a password, forgot it before the desk finished its first hum, and invented another, which the app described as "too similar" to the first. I had not known that a machine could be disappointed in my imagination, but there it was.

On the first morning the desk rose to its full height of 48 inches and stayed there. I did not ask it to. I do not think it asked itself. It simply went up, like a man who has been told to stand for the national anthem and then discovers the band has gone home.

It would not come down for three days.

During those three days I answered my correspondence standing on a kitchen chair, which is what you get for buying furniture that is smarter than you. A kitchen chair is a fine place to sit. It is a poor place to stand, and a worse place to write to your bank. My letters took on a wobbling, apologetic tone, and I believe some of them promised things I have not yet been able to deliver.

I called the company's help line. A pleasant voice listened to my account of the matter with great sympathy and then told me to "restart the furniture." I asked whether she meant the desk. She said she meant the furniture. I have thought about this exchange often, and I still do not know what restarting a chair would involve, though I now regard my own sofa with a certain caution.

It was my neighbor, Mrs. Abernathy, a retired schoolteacher, who solved the problem. She came over, looked at the desk for about as long as she once looked at boys who had not done their homework, bent down, pulled the plug out of the wall, and put it back. The desk shuddered, sighed, and descended. It did so with the humility of a pupil who knows he has been found out.

I thanked her. She said it was nothing. I felt that it was something, and that the something was my pride, which lay on the floor beside the plug.

I now work at the desk sitting down. I keep it at its lowest setting, where it is just a table, and where it has the decency to behave like one. The app remains on my phone. I do not open it. It has my fourteen characters, whatever they were, and I have decided to let it keep them.

My doctor asked me last week how the standing is going. I looked him in the eye, as a man should, and told him it is going well. This is true in the sense that something is going, and a desk is certainly in the room. I did not say who is sitting. He did not ask. We are both, I think, satisfied with the arrangement, which is how most medicine works.
