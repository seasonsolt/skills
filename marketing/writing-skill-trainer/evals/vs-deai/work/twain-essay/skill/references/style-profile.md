# Style Profile — Deadpan First-Person Humor Essay

Read this before drafting. It describes *mechanisms*: how the humor is built. It is not a phrasebook. All examples below are freshly written illustrations on invented subjects; never reuse their facts, numbers or wording in an essay, and never mention these rules in the essay itself.

Rule ids in brackets point to `reports/evidence-ledger.md`.

## 1. Narrator stance (the engine)

**1a. Hold a wrong premise in complete earnest.** [R-STANCE-01]
Choose one inverted or absurd conclusion that the supplied facts can be bent toward (the safe thing is the dangerous thing; the vice is a neglected craft; the failure is a record). The narrator believes it from the first sentence to the last. He never winks, never says "of course I'm joking", never laughs at his own line. The reader does all the laughing.

**1b. Misread your own setbacks as achievements, prudence or bad luck.** [R-STANCE-02]
The narrator is confident, a little vain, and wrong about what his experience means. A failure becomes a personal best; an absence of disaster becomes a disappointing streak of bad luck; a humiliation becomes evidence of good judgment.

> Fresh example: *I had bought the rowing machine to improve my health, and in this it succeeded at once, for I have never been so rested as in the three weeks I spent deciding where to put it.*

**1c. Point the absurdity at a real foible.** [R-TARGET-01]
Under the upside-down conclusion there should be something recognisable and true: people's polite pretences, alarmist reporting, the body's refusal to learn, official procedures that defeat their own purpose. Find the foible that the supplied facts already imply. If the pack implies none, aim the satire at the narrator's own misplaced confidence. Do not invent a social claim to have a target.

## 2. How facts carry the joke

**2a. Concrete beats adjectival.** [R-FACT-01]
The laugh lands on a specific supplied fact stated flatly — a count, a measurement, a date, a named object, a small exact detail — not on words like "hilarious", "disastrous", "absurd". Put the specific fact at the point of maximum tension, then stop.

> Fresh example: *The instructions promised assembly in under an hour. I can report that this was true of the box.*

**2b. Misframe, never misstate.** [R-FACT-02]
Every supplied fact stays exactly true. What goes wrong is the narrator's interpretation of it: the wrong comparison, the wrong cause, the wrong moral. Never change a number, date or name to make a joke work.

**2c. Mock-rigorous reasoning.** [R-LOGIC-01]
March the reader from plausible premise to absurd conclusion in small steps joined by reasoning words (so, therefore, it follows, plainly). Each step should feel almost reasonable on its own. Engines that work: a syllogism from a common saying; a comparison of two supplied numbers taken one step too far; a "law" derived from one event and applied to an unrelated part of life.
- Use only supplied facts as premises.
- Do **not** produce new figures. No sums, products, percentages, averages or projections that are not already in the pack. Compare supplied numbers in words ("the smaller of the two", "the same number again") rather than computing a new one.
- If the pack has no numbers, reason by sequence or category instead of arithmetic.

**2d. Mock authority.** [R-AUTH-01] *(conditional)*
When the pack contains an expert, an official, a manual, a sign, a record or a saying, the narrator may cite it earnestly as support for the absurd premise. If the pack has no such source, the narrator's own observation is his only evidence. Never invent a source, a study, a statistic or a quotation.

## 3. Structure and progression

**3a. Openings are free.** [R-OPEN-01]
No fixed opening. Choose from what the pack offers:
- an odd exchange or request that makes sense only later (only if the pack supplies the words exchanged, or the narrator's own words);
- a decision already made, announced as if it were obviously sound, before the reader learns what it cost;
- a clarifying distinction that sets up the premise (not this, but that).
Do not open with a definition, a rhetorical question about the topic, or a general statement about "life".

**3b. Repetition with escalation.** [R-ESC-01]
When the pack gives several attempts, items, days or incidents, present them as a series where one variable changes each time and the outcome stays the same or gets worse. The narrator's calm should grow as the damage grows. The third or later repetition is where the biggest laugh usually goes. If the pack has only one attempt, escalate the narrator's *interpretations* of it, not invented extra attempts.

> Fresh example shape: *The first time, I blamed the ladder. The second time I moved the ladder and blamed the wall. The third time my neighbor held the ladder, and we were both able to blame the wall together.* (Only if the pack supplies three attempts and a neighbor.)

**3c. From "I" to "you" when a lesson emerges.** [R-PERSON-01] *(conditional)*
If the material involves a procedure or a habit everybody shares, the essay may shift from the narrator's story to mock instructions for the reader ("you begin by…, and then you fall over") or to a mock-universal "we". Keep the instructions deadpan and accurate to the pack; each instruction can end in a deflation. If the material is purely personal, stay in "I".

**3d. Order of a typical essay** (a default, not a template):
odd entry → background facts delivered as if they were evidence → the reasoning or the escalating series → peak of mock alarm or mock triumph → a short resolution in which the narrator acts on his wrong conclusion → ending (§6).

## 4. Sentence rhythm and diction

**4a. Long run, short verdict.** [R-RHYTHM-01]
Build a long accumulating sentence — clauses chained with semicolons, dashes, lists that keep widening — and then follow it with a short, flat sentence of five to ten words that reports the outcome or the narrator's calm verdict. The short sentence is where the laugh lands. Vary the pattern; not every paragraph needs it.

> Fresh example: *I checked the forecast on two websites, consulted the barometer my aunt left me, studied the sky from the porch and again from the roof, and packed accordingly, with a scarf, a sun hat and an umbrella of the strongest kind. It snowed indoors.* (Illustration only; never add weather that is not supplied.)

**4b. Register mismatch.** [R-DICTION-01]
Describe trivial or low things in dignified, formal or technical language (a vice as a fine art; a toaster as a piece of engineering deserving respect). Describe genuine disasters in plain, mild, matter-of-fact words.

**4c. Understatement of harm.** [R-UNDER-01]
Pain, damage and embarrassment are reported as minor, routine, or even convenient. Only for harms that are in the pack; if the pack has no setback, skip this.

**4d. Homely comparisons.** [R-ANALOGY-01] *(knob: 0-3 per essay)*
An apt, deflating comparison to something domestic or animal (a new appliance as a nervous young animal; a meeting as a slow kitchen fire). Best placed at the end of a stretch. A comparison may not introduce a fact.

**4e. Emphasis.** At most one word per essay in italics or capitals for the key reversal. No bold, no exclamation marks except at a single peak of mock alarm.

**4f. Modern, plain vocabulary.** Write in present-day English. The humor comes from the stance and timing, not from old-fashioned words.

## 5. Asides and retreats [R-ASIDE-01] *(knob: 1-3 per essay)*

The voice talks, so it interrupts itself:
- a dash that breaks off a thought and rephrases it more discreetly;
- a bracketed note to the reader that points back to an earlier moment or comments on the narrator's caution;
- a mock-pedantic correction of a word;
- a discreet refusal to go into more detail, as if the details would be too much for the reader.
An aside may only comment on material already in the essay. It may never carry a new fact, name or number.

## 6. Endings [R-END-01]

End on a short reversal, a final mock instruction, or a moral that turns upside down and tilts toward the real foible of §1c. The last sentence should be one of the shortest or sharpest in the piece. Do not summarise; do not add a sincere lesson; do not explain the joke. If the pack offers no takeaway, end on the narrator's last confident misreading of a supplied fact.

## 7. Knobs (vary per essay; never treat as quotas)

| Knob | Range |
|---|---|
| Asides | 1-3 |
| Homely comparisons | 0-3 |
| Emphasized word | 0-1 |
| "You"-instruction section | only if the pack has a procedure or shared habit |
| Mock authority | only if the pack has a source |
| Paragraphs | whatever the content needs; short paragraphs for punchlines are fine |

## 8. Anti-patterns (check before delivery)

- **Winking**: "I know how this sounds", "jokes aside", "ironically", "funnily enough", laughing emoji or exclamations of amusement.
- **Explaining the joke** or ending with a sincere moral.
- **Period pastiche**: antique words, archaic spellings, old-fashioned contractions, nineteenth-century props or institutions that are not in the pack. Do not borrow the source author's era.
- **Source echo**: no sentences, distinctive phrases, famous lines or signature subjects of the source author. Do not name him or imply he wrote the piece.
- **Invented evidence**: new numbers (including computed ones), statistics, studies, experts, quotations, places, brands, people.
- **Adjective comedy**: calling things hilarious, absurd, ridiculous, chaotic instead of letting a fact show it.
- **Random absurdity**: surreal events that do not follow from the premise and the supplied facts.
- **Template collapse**: every essay opening the same way, always a stated moral, always a "you" section, always three repetitions.
- **Rule leakage**: never say "deadpan", "escalation", "register", "aside", "mechanism" or any rule name in the essay.
- **Reproducing defects**: source typos, duplicated words or flattened paragraphs are not style.

## 9. Style self-check

1. Could a reader point to the one wrong premise the narrator holds sincerely throughout?
2. Does at least one laugh land on a supplied concrete fact stated flatly?
3. Is there a reasoning chain or an escalating series built only from supplied facts?
4. Do long runs resolve into short flat sentences at least a few times?
5. Is harm understated and trivia dignified?
6. Does the ending turn, rather than summarise?
7. Is there a real foible under the joke?
8. Is every sentence free of winks, period words and source echoes?
